# The Questions the Allegation Makes Unavoidable

The first two documents do not ask the reader to decide negligence, motive or causation.

They do something more basic. They identify a management frame, a patient challenge to that frame, a later discharge artefact that preserved the course taken without preserving the challenge, and subsequent treatment evidence that repeatedly bore on the unresolved feet.

If that account is substantially accurate, the next task is not to restate it. It is to answer the questions it creates.

## 1. What exactly did the Protest require Neurology to reconsider?

The patient's evidence is that he did not reject the finding of Parkinsonism. He objected that the feet — the problem through which the illness had been disabling him and which had helped bring him to Neurology — remained unresolved.

That objection matters because the plan already had two practical consequences: dopaminergic treatment was to be deferred, while the chronic painful feet remained causally "Not clear" and were directed toward Podiatry.

The question is therefore not whether the patient personally demanded a levodopa trial. It is narrower and stronger:

> **Once the patient expressly challenged the adequacy of the foot disposition, what did reasonable neurological reconsideration of the existing plan require?**

Did it require nothing more than reassurance and continuation? Did it require the unresolved feet to be put back before senior neurological review? Did it require a more explicit account of why the feet were being kept outside the neurological explanation? Did it create a reason to use treatment response, examination or further movement-disorders assessment to test the separation?

The answer is ultimately an expert clinical question. But the existence of the question follows from the Protest itself.

## 2. What happened to the Protest between the bedside and the completed discharge artefact?

This is the central documentary question.

The final discharge artefact was not a frozen note produced at the moment of physical discharge. It expressly says that it was intended to consolidate discussions, assist with debriefing and set out a clear treatment plan. It remained open after the patient left hospital, incorporated the 13 July DaTscan, and was subsequently finalised and signed.

The completed document records the diagnosis, the treatment rationale, the patient's understanding, the Podiatry disposition and the plan. It does not record the patient's challenge to the adequacy of that disposition.

The question is not answered merely by observing that the Protest is absent. Nor is it answered by the finished document's own description of the admission as coherent and understood.

The question is:

> **What information about the Protest reached the author of the final synthesis, when did it reach him, and why does the completed patient-facing consolidation contain the pathway that continued but not the fact that the pathway had been challenged?**

That requires provenance: timing, authorship, draft history if recoverable, the people present, and the relationship between the bedside event and the later text.

The document's ability to absorb later information does not prove why the Protest is missing. It does make the omission a real question rather than something that can be dismissed on the assumption that the letter was already complete.

## 3. Were levodopa deferral and "Not clear" merely simultaneous, or did they operate together as the management frame?

The allegation describes two durable features of the July plan: levodopa was deliberately deferred in a very young patient, while the chronic painful feet remained outside the settled neurological explanation as "Not clear".

A sceptical reader is entitled to ask whether these were simply two unrelated decisions concerning different problems.

That question should be answered from the actual course of management, not from terminology alone.

> **Did prolonged dopaminergic restraint leave an important source of diagnostic information unused at the same time that the feet were being managed separately, and did the separation of the feet reduce the perceived need to use neurological treatment or neurological reassessment to clarify them?**

It is not necessary to show that the clinicians consciously described those propositions as one "frame". The relevant question is whether they operated together in practice.

If the feet could be sent to Podiatry because their neurological relationship remained unresolved, while the treatment most likely to expose dopaminergic responsiveness was deliberately postponed, the two decisions may have had a combined practical effect even if they arose from different clinical reasons.

That effect requires expert assessment. It should not be assumed merely from simultaneity.

## 4. Which foot phenomena are continuous across the record, and which are genuinely different?

This is the most useful technical challenge raised by an independent reading.

The word **feet** cannot be allowed to do all the work.

The record contains genuine structural pathology: corns, calluses, pes planus, hammertoes and pressure-related pain. It also contains toe-clawing, dystonic-looking lower-limb posture, abnormal gait, restlessness, an outside-left-foot phenomenon, later cramping and OFF phenomena, and chronic pain that changed with dopaminergic treatment.

The allegation does not require all of those things to have one cause.

It does require careful continuity analysis.

> **Which elements of the July 2017 unresolved foot problem are the same, overlapping with, or materially related to the phenomena that later changed with procyclidine, withdrawal, levodopa and OFF states?**

The October 2017 withdrawal evidence — "my feet are restless and my walk is falling apart" — and the April 2018 observation that the outside-left-foot phenomenon was the first thing to return rapidly are potentially powerful because they place the feet inside a treatment-state change.

But their significance depends on identifying what changed. A corn cannot disappear and regrow over a short medication interval. Pain, posture, loading, toe position, dystonic activity and gait can change much more rapidly.

The right question is therefore not "were the feet neurological?" It is:

> **What part of the foot syndrome was dynamically neurological, what part was structural, and what interaction existed between the two?**

That is the question July 2017 left "Not clear".

## 5. Once neurological modifiability of the feet became visible, where is the global reconsideration?

This is the longitudinal question on which the allegation ultimately stands or falls.

By April 2018 the new information was no longer confined to the patient's private experience. Neurology itself recorded the rapid return of the outside-left-foot phenomenon when procyclidine was withdrawn.

Later records recognised individual foot or leg phenomena as parkinsonian, dystonic or OFF-related. Levodopa and Stalevo altered pain and function. By 2025 the neurologist accepted that the feet felt better once Sinemet reached the system and later described the Sinemet CR relief as profound.

At the same time, peripheral neuropathy remained conditional: "If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here..."

That single phrase cannot by itself prove uninterrupted survival of the July 2017 model. The intervening record has to do that work.

The unavoidable question is:

> **Where, if anywhere, did Neurology return to the overarching July question and reconstruct the relationship between the chronic painful feet and the neurological disorder in light of the accumulating treatment-response evidence?**

If such a reconsideration occurred, it should be identifiable in the record and assessed on its reasoning.

If it did not occur, while increasingly neurological explanations were accepted locally, then "local update without global update" is not merely a phrase. It describes the longitudinal behaviour of the record.

## What now needs proof

The first two documents have done enough to identify the shape of the case. The next stage is proof rather than further description.

The key evidential work is therefore to establish the Protest and its audience; reconstruct the provenance and lifecycle of the discharge synthesis; obtain independent neurological evidence on what reconsideration the Protest and later treatment responses reasonably called for; map the foot phenomena precisely enough to distinguish structural pathology from dynamic neurological expression; and identify every point in the later record at which the overarching "Not clear" relationship was actually reconsidered, if it was.

The allegation should survive or fail on those questions.

It should not depend on repeating the allegation more loudly.

## The central institutional question

If the Protest occurred substantially as described, the hardest factual question is not simply why levodopa was not started.

It is:

> **What happened to a patient's express challenge to the adequacy of the plan between the bedside and a later-finalised document whose stated purpose was to consolidate discussions, debrief the patient and communicate that plan?**

## The central clinical question

The mirror-image question for the allegation is equally important:

> **Can the record demonstrate that the later treatment-responsive foot phenomena were sufficiently continuous with the unresolved July presentation that the original "Not clear" was being tested by the evidence, rather than merely supplemented by new and different symptoms?**

Those two questions now define the next evidential stage.
