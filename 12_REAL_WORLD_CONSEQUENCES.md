# Real World Consequences

**Purpose:** This document asks what follows if the allegation already set out in this repository is substantially correct. It does not determine negligence, motive or causation. It identifies the features of the case that become hardest to explain once the documentary chronology is placed beside the real clinical environment in which the decisions were made.

The case is unusual because its first year can initially be read as an impressive display of medicine working.

A GP begins with painful feet and abnormal gait, recognises that the presentation is becoming neurological, and refers urgently with an open differential. Neurology rapidly identifies a genuine young-onset Parkinsonian syndrome. The examination is detailed enough to recognise an unusual lower-limb posture that resembles foot drop while expressly stating that it is **“NOT FOOT DROP”** and is somewhat suggestive of dystonia. The team considers recessive young-onset Parkinsonism, including **PRKN/PARK2**. A deliberate treatment strategy is adopted because of the patient's age. Procyclidine and rasagiline produce a dramatic restoration of movement and function. Withdrawal then produces deterioration, including the feet and gait. By April 2018 the treating neurologist himself records that the first phenomenon to return rapidly off procyclidine is the outside-left-foot problem.

By that point the case should, in one sense, have been becoming easier.

The disease process was increasingly legible. The treatment was informative. The patient was functioning dramatically better. The unresolved feet had become demonstrably treatment-state dependent.

The question raised by the later history is therefore not primarily:

> **How was the disease missed?**

It is:

> **How did a case that was becoming increasingly understandable become harder to understand over time?**

If the allegation is substantially correct, five consequences are particularly difficult to reconcile with an ordinary account of clinical uncertainty.

---

## 1. The disease and the phenotype were both present, but they were not fully allowed to meet

The July 2017 record did not merely contain generic Parkinsonism.

It contained a striking young-onset, relatively symmetrical, akinetic-rigid syndrome; a gait resembling foot drop but specifically described as a **dystonic posture**; and an explicit genetic discussion asking whether the phenotype indicated **PRKN/PARK2**.

Yet when the same long account lists the patient-specific features supporting PRKN, it identifies very young onset, akinetic rigidity, absence of a dominant family history, marked symmetry and preserved cognition. The dystonic lower-limb finding is not carried into that list.

At the same time, the painful feet remain in another explanatory compartment: perhaps completely separate, perhaps secondary peripheral neuropathy, **“Not clear.”**

This is important because the problem is not failure to notice either ingredient.

The record contains the dystonic-looking gait.

The record contains PRKN.

What it does not visibly do is use the dystonic lower-limb phenotype to modify the PRKN formulation, or use the PRKN formulation to modify the meaning of the feet.

In miniature, that is the partition.

The real-world clinical environment makes a simple lack-of-knowledge explanation particularly difficult. Before this case, clinicians in the Galway Neurology environment were already publishing on Parkinson genetics, including PRKN. In August 2017, clinicians from the same Neurology Department — including Patrick Browne and Timothy Counihan — published a clinical-reasoning case involving a young patient whose abnormal foot posture appeared like foot drop, was classified as dystonia, was subsequently linked to compound-heterozygous **parkin** mutations, and was treated with procyclidine.

That external history does not prove that any named clinician personally read every line of this patient's chart or personally made the disputed foot decision.

It establishes something narrower and more consequential:

> **the relevant concepts were not remote from the department in which this case was being managed.**

If the case is as alleged, the difficult fact is therefore not that an obscure disease went unrecognised. It is that **the diagnosis and a clinically meaningful part of its phenotype appear in the same institutional environment — and even in the same long-form account — without being fully integrated.**

---

## 2. The patient challenged the unresolved feet at the precise moment the management account was hardening

The patient gives direct evidence that before discharge he objected that the feet remained unresolved.

On his account, four medical students were present; one was asked to fetch Dr Monaghan; and he used the NCT / bald-tyres analogy to make a simple point: an extensive neurological work-up had found something important, but the part of the problem through which he had reached medical attention had still not been dealt with.

That objection did not require the patient to know the final mechanism.

He did not have to say “PRKN dystonia”.

He did not have to demand levodopa.

He did not have to dispute Parkinsonism.

The objection was more elementary:

> **the proposed disposition did not answer the feet.**

If that account is accurate, it placed another course before Neurology: continue with the plan already formed, or return the unresolved feet and the patient's objection to senior neurological consideration before closure.

The significance of the omission is magnified by the lifecycle of the discharge artefact.

The completed account was not a frozen bedside note created at the instant of physical discharge. It expressly describes itself as a document intended to **consolidate discussions**, assist with **debriefing**, and set out a **clear plan**. It remained open after the patient left hospital, incorporated the later DaT scan, and was subsequently finalised and signed.

What survives is therefore a retrospective, patient-facing synthesis in which the treatment strategy is explained, the patient is represented as having a good understanding, the team has obtained a “firmer footing”, Podiatry forms part of the onward plan, and the recommendations are incorporated by reference to the preceding account.

What does not survive is the event which, on the patient's evidence, said:

> **this part of the plan is not settled.**

That is why the omission matters beyond ordinary incompleteness.

If the Protest occurred substantially as described, **the missing event is the event that would have told every later reader that the apparent settlement was contested at the point of creation.**

The allegation does not need to decide why the Protest is absent in order to identify that documentary consequence.

---

## 3. Corrective information kept arriving, but the original partition proved capable of absorbing it

The strongest challenge to a simplistic version of this case is also one of the strongest facts supporting the more precise allegation.

Neurology did not spend eight years insisting that nothing about the feet was neurological.

The record became more sophisticated.

That is exactly the point.

Procyclidine withdrawal produced rapid return of a focal foot phenomenon and deterioration in gait. A later foot throw was expressly called **parkinsonian**. Cramping prompted consideration of Parkinsonian dystonia. OFF dystonia was considered more likely than ON dystonia. A short-stepped protective gait was treated as typical OFF Parkinsonism. Stalevo facilitated activity and was used by the patient partly for pain. Later nursing assessment regarded leg pain and shuffling as OFF-related. Sinemet was recognised as making the feet feel better.

These are genuine neurological reintegrations.

But they are principally **local** reintegrations.

The chronic painful-foot syndrome could still retain a separate or unresolved underlying explanation. A particular movement could be parkinsonian. A cramp could be dystonia. Walking could fail in an OFF state. Sinemet could make the feet feel better. Parkinsonism could amplify or diminish pain.

None of those propositions necessarily forced the remaining proposition to change:

> **the underlying pain may still come from somewhere else.**

This is what **local update without global update** means in this case.

It does not mean that clinicians failed to recognise neurological foot phenomena.

It means that the model was elastic enough to recognise them **without having to reconstruct the overarching causal status of the chronic painful feet**.

The 2023 material is important precisely because it proves how sophisticated that accommodation became. The neurologist could regard substantial parts of the lower-limb picture as Parkinsonian or OFF-related while the pain continued to sit inside a mixed peripheral/neuropathic/other-cause model.

That may ultimately prove to have been clinically justified.

But it means the expert question is no longer whether Neurology “noticed” neurological responsiveness.

They plainly did.

The expert question becomes:

> **At what point, if any, did the accumulating neurological responsiveness require the original chronic painful-foot model itself to be reconstructed rather than continually supplemented?**

---

## 4. The levodopa caution was real — which makes the eventual success of timing and formulation more consequential

The long-term caution around levodopa was not imaginary.

The patient was unusually young. Dyskinesia and other dopaminergic complications were genuine concerns. Later experience gave those concerns clinical substance. It would be wrong to rewrite the case as though repeated dose escalation carried no cost.

But that does not end the inquiry.

It sharpens it.

By March 2025 the clinical record already described a striking temporal pattern: extreme pain on waking, the first steps being exceptionally difficult, and the feet improving when Parkinson medication reached the system. The neurologist accepted that more Sinemet would probably provide further relief but remained concerned about dyskinesia.

The intervention that followed did not abandon the caution strategy.

It changed **coverage**.

Controlled-release Sinemet was introduced at night in the setting of the severe morning experience. The later complaint response describes the result as **“profound relief.”**

That creates a real-world question which is different from “why was more levodopa not given?”

It is:

> **Was the management problem framed for too long principally as the amount of dopaminergic treatment, when timing, duration and formulation might also have been clinically important?**

The successful 2025 intervention is striking because it worked *inside* the existing caution.

It did not require an indiscriminate increase in daytime dosing or abandonment of dyskinesia concerns. A relatively modest alteration in the temporal distribution of levodopa produced a result profound enough to cause the patient to reinterpret the history of his feet.

The record may ultimately contain good clinical reasons why that solution was not appropriate or apparent earlier.

But if it does, those reasons need to be shown.

The result cannot simply be treated as ordinary continuity without asking what the success of the intervention says about the preceding management problem.

---

## 5. The final response relies on continuity in the very history whose construction is under challenge

This may be the most difficult consequence of all.

The March 2025 relief did not merely make the patient feel better.

It changed the question he was asking about the past.

Within days he filed a complaint. At that stage he expressly acknowledged that he did not yet possess all of the internal records. His theory was incomplete and, in several respects, would later change. But the bodily observation was already powerful enough to make him ask how so much pain could have persisted if this degree of relief was possible.

Dr Monaghan's response is therefore exceptionally important.

He does not answer by saying that the old 2017 reasoning is no longer relevant.

He does the opposite.

He points the reviewer back to his historical letters and says that they provide the documentary record of how decisions were made. He reproduces the original levodopa-caution passage and specifically highlights it in 2025. That passage includes the remarkable 2017 acknowledgement that the patient might do very well on levodopa and that:

> **“we may regret that we did not start this earlier.”**

The complaint response then restates the continuity of the treatment strategy: Sinemet responsiveness was expected; commencement and escalation were deliberately restrained in the young patient; Sinemet was known to help the pain; Lyrica was used for neuropathic pain **“of whatever cause”** partly to spare further Sinemet increase; Lyrica had incomplete effect at maximum dose; Sinemet CR was then introduced cautiously and produced **“profound relief.”**

And yet the same response retains:

> **“If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here…”**

and explains the Sinemet effect as relief of pain **“of whatever cause.”**

That is why the complaint response is not merely another later letter.

It is the mature articulation of the frame.

The treating neurologist accepts the local facts:

- Sinemet changes the feet;
- this has been known for some time;
- more Sinemet could produce more relief;
- Sinemet CR has now produced profound relief.

But the underlying chronic pain can remain causally separate.

The historical record is then invoked to show that this is continuity of a long-understood management strategy.

If the allegation is correct, that produces an extraordinary circularity:

> **the founding account omitted the patient's challenge to the foot disposition; the management course then developed inside the account that survived; and when the resulting course was challenged eight years later, the continuity of that same documentary history was relied upon as evidence that the course had been coherent all along.**

That is not proof that the original management was wrong.

It is the reason the original management can no longer be assessed merely by pointing to its later continuity.

---

## The real-world consequence

Put together, this is not best described as a missed diagnosis.

The right disease was substantially suspected.

The relevant phenotype was partly seen.

The genetic possibility later confirmed was already within the differential.

The treatment produced dramatic information.

The withdrawal produced corrective information.

Later clinicians increasingly recognised individual foot phenomena as neurological.

The patient gives direct evidence that he challenged the unresolved feet at the point of discharge.

And the relevant clinical environment possessed substantial movement-disorders and Parkinson-genetics expertise.

Yet the original partition appears to have been sufficiently durable that the record could become **more knowledgeable without necessarily becoming more integrated**.

That is the real-world consequence of a hard frame.

A hard frame does not require clinicians to ignore contrary evidence.

It can do something more subtle:

> **it can tell them where the new evidence belongs.**

A dystonic movement can belong to Parkinsonism.

An OFF gait can belong to Parkinsonism.

A medication-responsive pain fluctuation can belong to Parkinsonism.

A structural corn can belong to Podiatry.

A painful foot can still have another underlying cause.

And therefore every observation can be true while the original unresolved relationship survives.

The allegation to be tested is consequently narrower than “Neurology got the feet wrong.”

It is:

> **A management frame formed in July 2017 proved sufficiently durable to survive the information that should have tested it. The record progressively integrated individual neurological manifestations while preserving an unresolved or separate causal category for the chronic painful feet. When controlled-release levodopa finally produced profound relief in 2025, the treating neurologist was still able to explain the result within that inherited frame and to rely upon the continuity of his own historical documentation as evidence that the management course had been coherent.**

If that is what happened, the central expert question is no longer whether the clinicians were competent enough to recognise Parkinsonism, dystonia, OFF phenomena or medication response.

The record shows that they were.

The question is harder:

> **How did so much correct information enter the case without forcing the original relationship between the neurological disorder and the chronic painful feet to be rebuilt?**

That is the question raised by the real world of this case.
