# Real World Consequences

## The Claim, Stated at Full Strength

This is not a claim that Galway Neurology failed to recognise Parkinsonism.

It recognised Parkinsonism quickly and, in many respects, impressively.

It is not a claim that the department lacked movement-disorders expertise.

The opposite is true.

It is not even a claim that the department failed to notice neurological involvement in the feet over the years.

It noticed more and more of it.

The claim is harder.

> **In July 2017 Galway Neurology created a management plan with two durable parts: levodopa would be deliberately deferred because of the patient's very young age, and the chronic painful feet would remain outside the settled neurological explanation as “Not clear.” That plan became part of the anatomy of the case. Over the following eight years the department repeatedly acquired information that made the feet neurologically more intelligible, but the original unresolved partition remained available. By 2025 the department could acknowledge that Sinemet relieved the feet, acknowledge profound relief with Sinemet CR, acknowledge incomplete benefit from maximal pregabalin, and still preserve the underlying pain as potentially peripheral or “of whatever cause.”**

That is what has to be understood.

The remarkable feature of the case is not absence of knowledge.

It is the survival of a frame in the presence of knowledge.

---

## 1. The management plan was formed by Neurology, not discovered retrospectively by the patient

The long July 2017 account is not merely a collection of observations.

It is Neurology's own synthesis of what the admission meant and what would happen next.

The patient had a genuine and striking young-onset Parkinsonian syndrome. Because of his age and the anticipated risks of long-term dopaminergic treatment, levodopa and dopamine agonists were to be delayed. The document records senior neurological involvement in that strategy and states that these drugs were likely to be avoided **“for as long as possible.”**

The same treatment position appears in the surviving senior correspondence:

> **“Myself and Tim are keen to hold back on Levodopa insofar as maybe possible unless you'd advise otherwise?”**

The second part of the plan concerned the feet.

The GP had referred an integrated problem involving abnormal gait, feet, pathological tension, toe-clawing, movement-initiation difficulty, deteriorating dexterity and failing function.

Neurology saw a gait that looked almost like foot drop but expressly recorded:

> **“NOT FOOT DROP”**

and:

> **“somewhat suggestive of a dystonic posture.”**

It also recorded pes planus, hammertoes, corns and calluses and a longstanding painful foot history.

The extended synthesis then placed the painful foot phenomena into a separate unresolved category:

> **“may have been completely separate”**

or:

> **“perhaps a secondary peripheral neuropathy”**

and concluded:

> **“Not clear.”**

Podiatry was the practical destination.

These were not two unrelated phrases later joined together by an investigator.

They were two operative parts of the same forward management account:

> **Parkinsonism managed neurologically under prolonged dopaminergic restraint; chronic painful feet managed separately while their relationship to the neurological disorder remained “Not clear.”**

That is the originating frame.

---

## 2. The real-world provenance makes the frame difficult to dismiss as ordinary ignorance

The department in which this decision was made possessed unusually relevant expertise.

Timothy Counihan was a Consultant Neurologist with Galway University Hospitals and had served as President of the Irish Neurological Association.

More importantly, before this patient's admission he was a co-author of the 2013 *JAMA Neurology* paper:

> **“Parkin Disease: A Clinicopathologic Entity?”**

That paper dealt directly with PRKN/PARK2 disease as a form of juvenile and young-onset parkinsonism. It described dystonia among presenting signs, discussed **foot dystonia** as part of the proposed parkin phenotype, and described sustained low-dose levodopa responsiveness together with the tendency toward early dyskinesia.

Those are not remote similarities.

The patient's July 2017 record contained:

- very young-onset Parkinsonism;
- relative symmetry and akinetic rigidity;
- PRKN/PARK2 expressly under consideration;
- a lower-limb gait resembling foot drop but identified as dystonic rather than weak;
- deliberate caution about levodopa because of future dyskinesia risk.

Within weeks of this admission, clinicians from the same University Hospital Galway Neurology Department — Patrick Browne, Michael Hennessy, Patrick Cullinane and Timothy Counihan — published *Clinical Reasoning: A demure teenager and her dystonic foot*.

That case began with an abnormal foot posture giving the appearance of foot drop. It was classified phenomenologically as dystonia. Genetic testing later revealed compound-heterozygous **parkin** mutations. Treatment included procyclidine. The discussion states that parkin disease often responds very well to levodopa but that, because of the long expected treatment duration and dyskinesia risk, delaying levodopa with alternatives such as an anticholinergic may be prudent.

That is the real-world shadow across this case.

It does not prove that every named clinician personally reviewed every sentence in this patient's file.

It proves something more important for the institutional history:

> **Galway Neurology cannot plausibly be treated as a service in which the relationship between young-onset Parkinsonism, dystonic foot phenomenology, PRKN, procyclidine, levodopa responsiveness and dyskinesia risk was obscure or unavailable.**

The relevant knowledge was unusually close to the patient.

It was part of the intellectual environment of the department itself.

---

## 3. The partition is visible inside the founding document

The July account already contains almost all the pieces.

It contains dystonia.

It contains PRKN.

It contains structural feet.

It contains pain.

It contains the deliberate treatment strategy.

It contains the possibility of peripheral neuropathy.

But the pieces are not integrated symmetrically.

When the document lists the patient-specific features supporting PRKN, it identifies very young onset, akinetic rigidity, lack of a dominant family history, marked symmetry and preserved cognition.

The dystonic lower-limb phenomenon is not carried into that list.

Instead:

> **dystonia stays with the gait; PRKN stays with the Parkinsonian/genetic reasoning; the chronic painful feet stay with Podiatry / possible neuropathy / “Not clear.”**

This is not a retrospective theory imposed upon the document.

It is the internal architecture of the document.

The disease and the phenotype are both present.

They are not fully allowed to meet.

---

## 4. The patient challenged the frame before it became the durable account

Before discharge, the patient protested.

That is direct patient evidence.

His account is specific: four medical students were present; one was asked to fetch Dr Monaghan; and he used the NCT / bald-tyres analogy to explain that the extensive neurological work-up had found something important but had not dealt with the feet.

He was not rejecting Parkinsonism.

He was not demanding levodopa.

He was saying:

> **the plan does not answer the problem through which I came here.**

That matters because the plan already existed.

The Protest therefore created a choice:

> **continue with the management plan already formed; or return the unresolved feet and the patient's challenge to senior neurological reconsideration before closure.**

The first path continued.

The definitive discharge account does not preserve the second path as having existed.

And the discharge account was not a bedside snapshot already complete when the patient left hospital.

It described its own purpose as being to:

> **“consolidate a lot of discussions”**

assist the patient with:

> **“debriefing”**

and provide:

> **“a clear plan.”**

It later said the prolonged admission had produced a:

> **“firmer footing”**

for the plan, represented the patient as having a good understanding of the levodopa strategy, and converted the preceding narrative into the formal recommendations through:

> **“As per above.”**

The document then remained open after physical discharge, absorbed the 13 July DaTscan and later interpretation, and received later consultant finalisation.

So the important fact is not merely that a conversation is missing.

It is:

> **the event showing that part of the management settlement was contested did not enter the later-finalised document that consolidated and transmitted that settlement.**

That is how the frame hardened.

The issue here is documentary effect, regardless of why the omission occurred.

---

## 5. Then medicine supplied the information that should have made the unresolved part easier

The first year after admission is remarkable because the clinical story becomes progressively clearer.

The patient was discharged on rasagiline and procyclidine.

The response was dramatic.

He described his walk coming back together, his writing improving, his posture straightening and his legs becoming loose and limber. He could run, jump, hop and skip. He described the experience as:

> **“living in a miracle, literally.”**

Then came the planned procyclidine withdrawal.

The patient wrote:

> **“my feet are restless and my walk is falling apart.”**

By April 2018 this was no longer merely patient evidence.

The treating neurologist himself recorded that, when procyclidine was stopped, the **first thing** to change rapidly was the outside-left-foot phenomenon.

The same clinical review said the patient could be:

> **“very much better”**

with levodopa or another dopamine agonist, while retaining the deliberate policy of not using them for quite some time.

This is the calibration point in the entire longitudinal case.

A phenomenon in the very body region left **“Not clear”** was now demonstrably changing with neurological treatment withdrawal, and the treating neurologist knew it.

No later reconstruction is required to make that fact important.

The record itself contains it.

---

## 6. The frame did not remain static; it became capable of absorbing correction

This is where the claim must be exact.

Galway Neurology did not spend the next seven years saying that nothing about the feet was neurological.

That would be false.

The service increasingly recognised neurological components.

A foot throw was later described as **actually parkinsonian**.

Cramping and clawing raised Parkinsonian dystonia.

OFF dystonia was considered.

A protective short-stepped gait was understood as OFF Parkinsonism.

Stalevo facilitated movement and was used partly for pain.

Parkinson's nursing review connected severe leg pain and shuffling with under-treatment/OFF state.

By 2023 the clinical reasoning was already sophisticated enough to say that Parkinsonism could worsen the experience of pain arising from another source.

These were real updates.

But they did not abolish the original partition.

They refined it.

A movement could be Parkinsonian.

A cramp could be dystonic.

A gait failure could be OFF.

A dose of levodopa could improve the feet.

At the same time the underlying chronic painful-foot syndrome could remain peripheral, neuropathic, structural or otherwise separate.

That is how a hard frame survives evidence.

It does not have to reject the evidence.

It only has to decide where the evidence belongs.

---

## 7. The frame became part of the department's own anatomy

This is not a story in which one isolated 2017 opinion disappeared and unrelated clinicians unknowingly started again from zero.

The same neurological service continued to own the Parkinsonian management pathway.

The record shows the feet repeatedly re-entering that service through gait, dystonia, OFF state, medication timing, pain and treatment response.

The parallel Podiatry record was also capable of recognising neurological involvement. A 2019 re-referral explicitly linked previous foot problems to increased tone and abnormal gait associated with Parkinson's, described young-onset Parkinson's with **“clawing feet”** and pain, and visibly marked **“No neuropathy.”**

By 2021 levodopa was introduced while procyclidine was being removed.

By 2023 Neurology was expressly discussing Parkinsonian dystonia and OFF-related lower-limb symptoms.

By 2025 it was accepted that the feet improved when Sinemet reached the system.

The claim is not that every clinician who touched the case personally knew every historical detail.

The claim is that, by this stage, it becomes highly implausible to describe the persistent neurological relevance of the feet as information unavailable to the service.

It was being generated repeatedly **inside the service's own care**.

The frame survived not because the department saw nothing.

It survived while the department saw more.

That is what it means to say the frame became part of the anatomy of the case.

---

## 8. 2025 reveals the mature form of the original “Not clear”

By March 2025, the remaining architecture is almost explicit.

The patient had severe morning foot pain.

The first steps were described as torture.

The feet improved when Parkinson medication reached the system.

The neurologist expected more Sinemet to produce more pain relief but remained concerned about dyskinesia.

Controlled-release Sinemet was used to extend treatment coverage.

The result was later described by the treating neurologist as:

> **“profound relief.”**

Yet the same clinical reasoning retained:

> **“If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here…”**

This is not merely an interesting choice of words.

It is the mature form of **“Not clear.”**

The neurological connection is now accepted.

The underlying causal status remains conditional.

Sinemet can improve the feet without the feet having to be brought fully inside the neurological cause.

That is exactly the architecture established in 2017, only more sophisticated.

---

## 9. The complaint response proves ownership and continuity of the frame

The 25 March 2025 complaint response is the most important later document because Dr Monaghan himself supplies the genealogy.

He does not say the 2017 framework is obsolete.

He points the reviewer back to it.

He says his clinical reasoning will be evident to the reviewer.

He says the purpose of his long letters is to provide clear documentation of:

> **“how decision were made and when and by who.”**

He says the information in his present account and:

> **“all of the letters from 2017”**

should provide the answers.

Then he expressly re-adopts the old treatment rationale.

He reproduces the 2017 passage saying the patient understood why levodopa was being delayed and repeats the historical acknowledgement:

> **“we may regret that we did not start this earlier.”**

He adds bolding and underlining in 2025.

He then describes the longitudinal management in collective terms:

> **“we have known”**

that the condition would be Sinemet/dopamine-responsive;

> **“we have tried”**

to spare the commencement and escalation of those drugs;

and:

> **“we have had a clear understanding for quite some time that Sinemet does help his pain.”**

He explains that Lyrica was introduced for neuropathic pain:

> **“(of whatever cause) in part to try to spare further Sinemet increase”**

acknowledges:

> **“incomplete effect at maximum dose”**

and then acknowledges that Sinemet CR produced:

> **“profound relief currently.”**

Yet he retains the same underlying architecture:

> **“If there is a degree of peripheral neuropathy here…”**

and pain:

> **“of whatever cause.”**

This is not the patient retrospectively inventing continuity.

The treating neurologist himself claims continuity.

He takes ownership of the old letters, the old rationale, the later restraint, the known pain response and the final Sinemet CR result and presents them as one coherent course.

That is why the 2025 response is so important.

It gives the management frame its genealogy in the clinician's own words.

## 10. What the real world does to this case

This is where the institutional context casts its shadow.

If this had occurred in a service with little movement-disorders exposure, one possible explanation would be that the significance of the phenotype simply escaped notice.

That explanation is difficult here.

The department had senior expertise in young-onset Parkinsonism, dystonia and PRKN.

Its clinicians were publishing on the exact conceptual territory.

The patient's own file repeatedly supplied the relevant phenomena.

The patient challenged the unresolved disposition.

The first year supplied treatment-response information.

Later years supplied Parkinsonian, dystonic and OFF-state interpretations.

Levodopa itself eventually supplied further information.

By 2025 the neurological service openly accepted that Sinemet improved the feet.

The problem therefore cannot credibly be reduced to:

> **nobody knew.**

Nor can it be reduced to:

> **nobody noticed.**

Too much was known and too much was noticed.

The harder proposition is that the original management distinction had become sufficiently established that the department could continue to place each new fact inside it without having to rebuild it.

That is the shadow the real-world expertise casts over the chronology.

It makes simple ignorance less plausible.

It makes the persistence of the frame more consequential.

---

## The claim

The claim is now straightforward.

> **Galway Neurology formed a two-part management plan in July 2017. Dopaminergic replacement would be deliberately deferred in a very young patient, while the chronic painful feet would remain outside the settled neurological explanation as “Not clear.” The department possessed unusually relevant expertise in PRKN, dystonia and young-onset Parkinsonism and had already documented a dystonic-looking lower-limb phenotype in this patient. The patient directly challenged the unresolved disposition before discharge, but that challenge did not enter the later-finalised account which consolidated and transmitted the plan. Within months, treatment and withdrawal demonstrated neurological modifiability of the feet, and Neurology itself recorded the rapid return of the outside-left-foot phenomenon. Over the following years the department progressively recognised particular foot and lower-limb phenomena as Parkinsonian, dystonic, OFF-related and levodopa-responsive, while preserving a separate or unresolved underlying category for the chronic pain. By 2025 Sinemet benefit was openly acknowledged, pregabalin had been used partly to spare further Sinemet, maximal pregabalin had been incompletely effective, Sinemet CR had produced profound relief, and peripheral neuropathy nevertheless remained conditional. The treating neurologist then used the continuity of his own documentation from 2017 onward to explain and defend the continuity of the management course.**

That is what happened in the record.

The question for expert review is not whether the department possessed the concepts necessary to understand the case.

It plainly did.

The question is:

> **How did a department possessing this expertise, repeatedly seeing this patient's treatment-state information, and progressively recognising neurological involvement in the feet continue for eight years without reconstructing the original unresolved relationship that its own founding document had called “Not clear”?**

That is not a claim of incompetence.

It is a claim about the durability of a management frame.

And the more closely the real clinical environment is examined, the harder that durability is to explain as mere lack of knowledge.
