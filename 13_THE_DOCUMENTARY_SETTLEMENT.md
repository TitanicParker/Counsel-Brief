# 13 — The Documentary Settlement

## The first answer after the Protest

The last time I saw Dr Thomas Monaghan during the July 2017 admission, I had called him back because I believed the feet — the problem through which I had come into medical attention — had still not been properly dealt with.

Four medical students were present.

I used the NCT / bald-tyres analogy because the point was simple:

> **You have brought me in, examined me extensively, found something important, and yet you are sending me out without having dealt with the part that brought me here.**

I was not rejecting Parkinsonism. I was not demanding levodopa. I was not presenting a competing diagnosis.

I was saying that the work-up had not answered the feet.

The last thing I remember is Dr Monaghan walking away through the curtain.

As far as I am concerned, the next thing he said to me was the long discharge account.

That changes how the document has to be read.

---

## 1. The document speaks directly to me

The long discharge account does not present itself as a terse technical note written only for other doctors.

It says part of its purpose is:

> **“for supply to Liam”**

It says it is intended:

> **“to consolidate a lot of discussions which we have had”**

It says it is meant to assist me:

> **“with debriefing”**

and:

> **“to set out for him a clear plan.”**

Those phrases identify the document as the neurologist’s explanation to me of what the admission meant.

The document is therefore not only a record of the admission.

It is also an answer.

And the obvious question is:

> **What is it answering?**

The last unresolved exchange between us concerned the feet.

I had said that the proposed disposition did not answer them.

The document that followed says it is for me, that it will consolidate our discussions, debrief me, and provide a clear plan.

Yet it does not record the discussion in which I challenged the adequacy of that plan.

---

## 2. The Protest created a choice before the document was finished

The significance of the Protest is not merely that I disagreed.

The Protest changed the decision environment.

Before it, Neurology could continue to regard the feet as a problem for Podiatry while the neurological work proceeded on its own terms.

After it, Dr Monaghan had been expressly told that I regarded that separation as inadequate.

At that point a choice existed.

He could continue with the existing plan.

Or he could stop and go back.

He could re-read the GP referral.

He could re-examine the feet.

He could ask why a man referred with painful abnormal feet, pathological tension, toe-clawing, gait abnormality and failing function had undergone an extensive neurological work-up without a comparable documented investigation of the painful feet themselves.

He could reconsider the dystonic-looking lower-limb posture already recorded.

He could return the question to the senior neurologists involved in the case.

He could delay discharge.

He could decide that Podiatry remained sufficient, but record why.

He could preserve the disagreement and create an explicit reopening condition:

> **if neurological treatment changes the feet, reconsider this relationship.**

None of those alternatives requires the proposition that levodopa had to be started immediately.

The point is more basic.

The Protest made continuation of the existing plan **a choice**.

---

## 3. What disappeared was not only the objection

The final discharge account does not record that choice.

If the Protest were present, the record would have to say, in substance:

> Liam objected that the feet remained unresolved.

And the next question would immediately be:

> **What did Neurology do about that?**

Did the feet get re-examined?

Was the referral reconsidered?

Was the question returned to senior review?

Was discharge delayed?

Was Podiatry reconsidered as sufficient or insufficient?

Was there a documented reason for continuing unchanged?

Once the Protest is recorded, continuation itself becomes a decision requiring explanation.

Without the Protest, that decision disappears.

The record can instead read as though the path from diagnosis to discharge was continuous and natural.

> **The omission did not simply erase the Protest. It erased the choice the Protest created.**

---

## 4. The document replaces the unresolved ending with a settled account

The discharge artefact says that the prolonged admission:

> **“has all suited us and Liam very well”**

and that it had given the team:

> **“a firmer footing going forward for having a plan”**

It describes me as highly intelligent, unusually insightful, capable of retaining information and able to question appropriately.

It later says:

> **“Liam has a good understanding of why we wished to delay the commencement of levodopa or dopamine agonist type treatments at least for now.”**

And the formal Plan / Recommendations section says:

> **“As per above.”**

So the document does more than record medical facts.

It describes my state of mind.

It tells the reader what I understood.

It tells the reader how well the admission had gone.

It tells the reader that a firm plan had emerged.

And it incorporates the preceding narrative into the formal management plan.

This is not a sparse note that simply omitted most bedside conversations.

This is an unusually long, personal, explanatory document which expressly claims to consolidate discussions and communicate the plan to me.

It has room for my intelligence.

It has room for my insight.

It has room for my questions.

It has room for what I supposedly understood.

It has room for how well the admission “suited” me.

What it does not contain is the discussion in which I said:

> **the plan has not answered the feet.**

---

## 5. The patient-facing purpose makes the omission more significant

The more the document says it exists for my understanding, the more significant the omission becomes.

If this had been an internal technical note, one might argue that it was never intended to preserve every exchange with the patient.

But that is not how this document describes itself.

It is expressly written partly for me.

It is intended to debrief me.

It is intended to consolidate our discussions.

It is intended to give me a clear plan.

The obvious contested issue requiring debriefing was the feet.

A genuinely responsive patient-facing account could have said:

> You raised concern before discharge that the feet remained unresolved. We reconsidered this and decided that Podiatry remained the appropriate next step because of the following reasons.

Or:

> You raised concern, and we have therefore returned the question to senior review.

Or:

> You raised concern, and although the neurological relationship remains uncertain, we will use treatment response and follow-up to test it.

Any of those would have preserved both the plan and the fact that I had challenged it.

Instead, the document communicates the plan without communicating the challenge.

The effect is to present the chosen course back to me as the coherent meaning of the admission.

---

## 6. The document was still open

The long discharge account was not frozen at the moment I physically left hospital.

It shows later transcription finalisation.

It incorporates the 13 July DaTscan.

It apologises for the delay in getting the document out.

It carries later consultant sign-off.

The document was therefore still capable of absorbing later information after the bedside interaction had ended.

It did absorb later diagnostic information.

It did not absorb the Protest.

So this cannot be reduced to:

> **the document had already been completed before the objection occurred.**

It had not.

---

## 7. The unresolved bedside ending becomes a documentary settlement

The chronology is simple.

The bedside ending was unresolved.

I had told the neurologist that the feet had not been dealt with.

The next communication from him was a document saying:

> **for supply to Liam**

> **consolidate a lot of discussions**

> **debriefing**

> **a clear plan**

The document does not preserve the unresolved ending.

It replaces it with a settled one.

> **The last thing I said to the neurologist was that the plan did not answer the feet. The next thing the neurologist said to me was a document telling me what the clear plan was.**

That is the sequence.

---

## 8. Why this matters to the later history

If the Protest had been recorded, later readers would have inherited a different history.

They would not merely know that Parkinsonism had been identified, levodopa was being deliberately deferred, the feet were “Not clear”, Podiatry was arranged, and the patient understood the plan.

They would also know:

> **the patient expressly challenged the adequacy of the foot disposition before discharge, and the existing plan was continued after that challenge.**

That single additional fact changes the meaning of every later development.

When procyclidine withdrawal made the outside-left-foot phenomenon return rapidly, a later clinician would be able to see that this bore directly on an unresolved question the patient had already forced into the open.

When Podiatry repeatedly treated the same left-lateral foot territory, the record would contain the earlier challenge to the separation.

When later foot phenomena were called Parkinsonian, dystonic or OFF-related, the reader would know that the neurological relationship had been contested from the beginning.

When Sinemet was introduced in 2021, the record would contain an obvious historical calibration question:

> **What does this new neurological treatment do to the foot phenomenon that had already been shown to change with neurological medication?**

When severe pain persisted despite high-dose pregabalin, the historical uncertainty would not look like an uncontested clinical inheritance.

And when Sinemet CR finally produced profound relief in 2025, the old record could no longer be invoked as though the original management course had simply unfolded without an alternative having been raised.

That is what the omission changes.

---

## 9. Continuity was not inevitable

The surviving record gives the impression of continuity:

> diagnosis → treatment strategy → Podiatry → follow-up → later medication → later pain management.

Restoring the Protest changes that chronology:

> diagnosis → feet remain unresolved → patient objects → another course becomes available → existing plan nevertheless continues.

The future was not inevitable at the moment of discharge.

There was a point at which the course could have been reopened.

The Protest marks that point.

The final discharge artefact does not.

> **What disappeared from the record was not only an objection. It was the moment at which the future ceased to be inevitable.**

---

## 10. The personal tone sharpens the contradiction

The author goes out of his way to describe me as intelligent, insightful and capable of asking appropriate questions.

He writes partly for my own understanding.

He says the document is meant to bridge the complexity of the admission and provide a clear forward plan.

Years later, he would describe his long letters as a way of ensuring shared understanding between clinician and patient and as a record of how decisions were made, when, and by whom.

That makes the missing exchange difficult to treat as immaterial.

The document is explicitly concerned with what I understood.

But it does not preserve what I challenged.

It is explicitly concerned with the discussions we had.

But it does not preserve the discussion that called the management plan into question.

It is explicitly concerned with how decisions were made.

But it does not preserve the decision point created by the Protest.

That is the contradiction.

---

# The finding

I say that the long July 2017 discharge account was the first substantive communication I received from Dr Monaghan after the NCT / bald-tyres Protest.

The last face-to-face interaction between us left the disposition of the feet expressly contested.

The next communication was a later-finalised document written partly for me, expressly intended to consolidate our discussions, assist with debriefing and set out a clear plan.

That document did not acknowledge the contested ending.

It did not record the choice the Protest had created.

It did not explain whether the feet had been reconsidered, whether senior review had been sought again, whether discharge had been reconsidered, or why the existing pathway was continued.

Instead, it presented a coherent account of diagnosis, patient understanding and forward management and incorporated that narrative into the formal plan.

The documentary effect was therefore larger than omission of a conversation.

> **An unresolved bedside ending became a documentary settlement.**

And that settlement mattered because it was the version of the admission that could travel forward.

---

# In one paragraph

> **I say that before discharge I directly challenged the fact that the feet — the problem through which I had originally come into medical attention — had not been adequately dealt with. That challenge created a real decision point: Neurology could continue with the plan already formed, or it could delay closure, return to the referral, re-examine the feet, seek further senior review, reconsider whether Podiatry was sufficient, or at minimum preserve the disagreement and define how the unresolved relationship would be revisited. The existing pathway continued. The next substantive communication I received from Dr Monaghan was the long discharge account, expressly written partly “for supply to Liam,” to “consolidate a lot of discussions,” assist with “debriefing,” and provide “a clear plan.” The document did not preserve the Protest or the decision point it created. Instead, it represented the admission as having produced a coherent, understood and forward-looking management settlement. The omission therefore did not merely remove my objection from the record. It removed the point at which continuation of the existing plan became one choice among alternatives, and replaced an unresolved bedside ending with documentary continuity.**
